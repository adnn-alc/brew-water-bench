Water for coffee, in ppm as CaCO3

Two numbers run your cup: hardness pulls flavor out, alkalinity mutes acid.

A working reference for building coffee water from distilled or RO: what each mineral does, published recipes on one map, and a mixer that works with whichever mineral bottles you own. Where the evidence is thin or people disagree, it says so.

Start here

New to this? Two bottles and one side-by-side tasting teach more than any chart. All you need is distilled or ZeroWater, Epsom salt, baking soda and a 0.01 g scale.

Make two concentrates

Dissolve 6 g of Epsom salt in distilled water and top up to 600 mL. Do the same with 6 g of baking soda in a second bottle. Each is now 10 g per litre. Keep the baking soda bottle in the fridge.

Check another bottle size

Mix a litre of 80 / 40

Weigh 19.7 g from the Epsom bottle and 6.7 g from the baking soda bottle, then top up to 1 kg with distilled water. Hardness 80 does the extracting. Alkalinity 40 keeps the acidity in check.

Change one number and taste

Brew the same coffee with this water and with plain distilled. Then move only the alkalinity: 25 should taste brighter, 55 rounder. When that difference is clear to you, do the same with hardness.

Filling an espresso machine? Skip the steps above and use rpavlis: potassium bicarbonate and nothing else, so there is nothing to scale or corrode.

What each mineral contributes

A salt always brings two ions. Epsom salt is magnesium plus sulfate; baking soda is sodium plus bicarbonate. The positive ions do the extracting and shaping, and bicarbonate does the buffering.

How solid is this?

The bicarbonate effect is the best supported: it neutralizes coffee acids, and tasters reliably find high-alkalinity water flat and chalky. The magnesium vs calcium story is weaker. Hendon's 2014 paper was a computer model of binding energies, not a tasting, and it found magnesium binds flavor compounds most strongly. But in Royal Coffee's blind tasting, tasters preferred a calcium water and three of five called the magnesium water flat, and Lance Hedrick's blind tests landed on calcium plus potassium. Jonathan Gagné notes there are no lab tests showing magnesium extracts different flavors. Treat the taste words on these tiles as tendencies people report, then trust your own cup.

How it fits together

GH: total hardness

The sum of calcium and magnesium. These doubly charged ions are the ones that grab flavor compounds in the grounds. Too little and the cup is thin and tea-like. Too much and it turns heavy, muddy or bitter.

KH: alkalinity

The bicarbonate content, which is the water's capacity to neutralize acid. It has the bigger effect on taste. Too little and acidity reads sharp or sour; too much and the cup goes flat and chalky. Royal Coffee found water with alkalinity equal to hardness tasted flat, and about half as much alkalinity as hardness did better. That 2:1 ratio is also the rule of thumb from Water for Coffee.

Why "ppm as CaCO3"

Different ions weigh different amounts, so chemists convert everything to the weight of calcium carbonate that would do the same job. It lets you add calcium and magnesium on one scale. It also means 80 ppm GH is not 80 mg/L of magnesium: it is about 19 mg/L.

Alkalinity hits filter much harder than espresso

A pour-over uses around 16 g of water per gram of coffee and an espresso around 2. Each gram of coffee meets roughly seven times more buffer in a filter brew. That is why filter recipes sit at 25 to 40 KH while espresso recipes run 45 to 55 and Scott Rao has enjoyed espresso at 200 to 300.

It is not linear

Rao dosed the same coffee to 10, 30, 50 and 70 KH. The 10 and 70 cups stood out and the middle two did not. Expect surprises, and compare cups side by side instead of predicting.

Minerals work after brewing too

Rao (citing Samo Smrke) and Apax Lab both report that coffee tastes essentially the same whether the minerals go in before or after brewing. That makes testing cheap: brew once with distilled water and dose individual cups.

Reference zones

SCA 2009 standard: calcium hardness 68 (17–85), alkalinity near 40, pH 6.5–7.5, sodium near 10 mg/L, TDS 150 (75–250).
SCA handbook 2018 zone: GH 50–175, KH 40–75.
La Marzocco machines: GH 70–100, KH 40–80, chloride under 30 mg/L, TDS 90–150, pH 6.5–8.

Two findings that cut against the folklore

In Royal Coffee's double-blind round on a high-scoring Mexican coffee, plain distilled water took the highest total score, with an SCA-spec calcium water a hair behind. And on Ethiopian washed and natural lots at three roast levels, calcium water was preferred nearly everywhere; only on the light washed coffee did magnesium keep pace. High bicarbonate lost on everything except the darkest roasts.

Water that goes inside an espresso machine

A boiler cares about three things: scale, corrosion, and having enough ions for the level probe to sense water. Taste comes second.

Scale

Ca²⁺ + 2 HCO₃⁻ → CaCO₃ ↓ + CO₂ + H₂O

Heat drives this reaction to the right, and limescale drops out on the hottest surfaces. It takes calcium and bicarbonate together. Magnesium carbonate is far more soluble, so magnesium with bicarbonate does not scale at normal recipe strengths. Remove calcium and the scale problem mostly disappears.

Corrosion

Chloride pits stainless steel, which is why La Marzocco caps it at 30 mg/L and why Apax and others say to keep chloride concentrates out of the machine. Sulfate is the milder concern and mostly discussed for copper. Water with almost no alkalinity is also corrosive: the SCA handbook puts the floor near 40 ppm KH at boiler temperatures.

The rpavlis recipe

Robert Pavlis, a chemistry professor on Home-Barista, proposed the simplest safe water: distilled water plus about 0.1 g/L potassium bicarbonate.

0.1 g/L KHCO₃ → KH 50 · GH 0 · potassium 39 mg/L No calcium or magnesium, so nothing to scale. No chloride or sulfate, so nothing to pit. 50 ppm of buffer protects the metal and feeds the level probe.

Potassium is used over sodium because it tastes less salty at the same alkalinity. Your setup already follows this.

Adding flavor without adding risk

  • Epsom salt on top of rpavlis: 0.1 g/L adds about 40 GH. Forum users run up to twice that without scale. It does add sulfate, which strict rpavlis avoids.
  • Or keep the tank on plain rpavlis and add hardness to the cup. Apax suggests 1 to 2 drops per 40 mL espresso; Lotus magnesium drops work the same way.
  • Bicarbonate stock solutions can grow bacteria. One coffee scientist advises keeping them only days; others report years without trouble. Refrigerate and remake if cloudy.

Rao's four-cup test

  1. Brew 900 mL of coffee with distilled water. Combine pour-overs or do one big immersion brew.
  2. Stir, then split into four 225 mL cups.
  3. Dose each cup differently. With Lotus drops at this size, each Ca or Mg drop adds 20 ppm GH and each Na or K drop adds 10 ppm KH. With 10 g/L concentrates, 1.1 g of Epsom adds 20 ppm GH and 0.4 g of baking soda adds 10 ppm KH.
  4. Stir and taste blind.

Start with big gaps, such as 10, 50, 100 and 150 KH at a fixed hardness. You may not be able to tell 10 from 30. Narrow the range on the next round, then repeat for GH.

Measuring

A TDS pen measures how well the water conducts electricity and multiplies by about 0.5 to show "ppm". It cannot tell hardness from alkalinity, and its number is not the mg/L of minerals: an 80 / 40 water holds about 162 mg/L by weight but reads near 130. The mixer and every recipe card show the reading to expect, worked out from each ion's conductivity, good to roughly 10%. Use the pen as a batch check: ZeroWater should read 000 before you start, and the same recipe should land on the same number every time. Aquarium GH and KH drop-titration kits can. If you only buy one, measure KH. Weigh salts on a 0.01 g scale, and remember Epsom salt is about half crystal water by weight, which every figure here already accounts for.
